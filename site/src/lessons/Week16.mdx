<BigPicture>

A robot is where every difficulty in this course arrives together. Experience is slow and wears out hardware. Mistakes break things. A task is useful only if it works nearly every time. So robot learning leans on two sources of cheap experience: a **simulator**, where a policy can practise for years in an afternoon, and a large **pretrained policy**, which already does most things passably. Each leaves a gap that reinforcement learning has to close. The week ends by putting the whole course on one page.

## Learning in simulation

<SimToRealLoop />

The simulator is wrong in two ways. Some errors are **parametric**: the right equations with the wrong numbers, such as mass, friction or motor strength. Others are **non-parametric**: effects the simulator does not model at all, such as air flow around a drone, a cable dragging, or a soft object deforming.

<FromSimToReal />

<TryThis>

1. The simulator assumed friction 0.40 and the real floor is 0.30. The policy tuned for 0.40 sends the puck 33 cm past the line. It was perfect in simulation.
2. The policy tuned across 0.20 to 0.60 is 9 cm long. The one that nudges first is 5 cm short, and that 5 cm is entirely its measurement error.
3. Move the real floor to 0.60. Now the range-tuned policy (46 cm short) is *worse* than the single-simulator one (33 cm short). One launch speed cannot suit every floor, so it settled on a cautious speed that avoids big overshoots on slippery floors and pays for it on sticky ones.
4. Sweep the real floor from 0.20 to 0.80 and watch the blue line stay flat. Adapting is the only one of the three that is right everywhere.
5. Widen the randomisation to ±0.30. The compromise gets more cautious still: 46 cm short even at 0.40, the floor it was centred on.
6. Raise the measurement error to 20%. The adaptive policy degrades gracefully, by about as much as it mismeasures.

</TryThis>

<PlainWords>

A policy trained in one simulator has learned that simulator. Randomising the simulator teaches it to cope with a family of worlds using one behaviour, which works when one behaviour can cope and becomes timid when it cannot. The step beyond is a policy that looks at how the world has been responding and adjusts, which is the meta-RL idea of Week 13 applied to physics.

</PlainWords>

A caution about the playground: its policy is a single number with no feedback, which makes randomisation look weaker than it is. Real policies react to what they sense at every step, and that alone absorbs a great deal of mismatch. The trade-off is real all the same.

### Three ways across the gap

| | Idea | In control terms | Weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Domain randomisation** | Train one policy across many simulators with different physics, sensing and delays | Robust control | Too wide a range gives a cautious policy |
| **Learning to adapt** | Let the policy infer the hidden physics from its recent history | Adaptive control | Needs the two-stage training below |
| **Real-to-sim** | Use real measurements to fix the simulator: identify its parameters, or learn a correction for what it gets wrong | System identification | Needs real data, and only fixes what was measured |

They combine, and most systems use all three.

<TeacherStudent />

The student's training is imitation learning from Week 8 with a perfect, always-available expert, so DAgger works without a human in the loop. A one-stage variant is the **asymmetric actor-critic**: the actor sees only what the real robot will sense, while the critic, needed only during training, is given the privileged information.

For tasks where even writing a reward is hard, such as whole-body humanoid motion, a common recipe borrows the task from people: record a human motion, **retarget** it to the robot's body, and train a policy in simulation to track it. Simulation supplies the physics that the human recording lacks.

## Improving a pretrained robot policy

The second source of cheap experience is other people's data. A **vision-language-action model** starts from a pretrained vision-language model and is trained by imitation on a large pool of robot demonstrations. It takes images and an instruction and outputs actions, usually through a diffusion or flow head that produces a short chunk of actions at once.

Like the language models of Week 15, it is good and not good enough. Imitation tends to level off around 80% success, and autonomy needs 99% or better.

<KeepWhatWorked />

<TryThis>

1. The pretrained policy succeeds 80.7% of the time. If a job has five such stages in a row, that is 34% end to end.
2. Step the rounds forward. Imitating every success creeps up: 85.7% after one round, 94.3% after eight (74% for five stages).
3. Imitating only better-than-average attempts reaches 91.4% after one round and 98.8% after two (94% for five stages).
4. Look at the bars to see why. A success with the 85% grasp is still a success, so the orange rule keeps rewarding it. The blue rule asks a sharper question: was this choice better than what the policy usually does?
5. Drop to 20 attempts per round. The blue rule now has to judge each way of grasping from a handful of tries, and sometimes throws away the best one by bad luck: on average it now levels off at 98.5%, short of the 99% ceiling. A value estimate is only as good as its data.

</TryThis>

<Derivation title="Why imitating your own successes is policy improvement" why="it shows the simplest RL fine-tuning recipe is sound, and why it is slow.">

Let the policy choose option $a$ with probability $p(a)$, and let $q(a)$ be that option's success rate. Its success rate is $J(p) = \sum_a p(a) q(a)$. Among successful attempts, option $a$ appears with probability

$$
p'(a) = \frac{p(a)\, q(a)}{\sum_b p(b)\, q(b)} = \frac{p(a)\, q(a)}{J(p)} .
$$

Fitting the policy to the successes gives $p'$, whose success rate is

$$
J(p') = \frac{\sum_a p(a)\, q(a)^2}{J(p)} = \frac{\mathbb{E}_p[q^2]}{\mathbb{E}_p[q]} \;\ge\; \mathbb{E}_p[q] = J(p),
$$

because $\mathbb{E}[q^2] \ge \mathbb{E}[q]^2$. The improvement is $\operatorname{Var}_p(q) / J(p)$: large when the options differ a lot, tiny when the remaining options are 99% and 85%. Weighting by success is a gentle push. Weighting by advantage, $q(a) - J(p)$, pushes harder, at the price of having to estimate it.

</Derivation>

At scale, the blue rule is a recipe built entirely from supervised learning, which is why it suits very large models:

1. **Collect** a large batch of the robot's own attempts, including moments where a person stepped in.
2. **Fit a value function** by regression, for example predicting how many steps remain until the task is done.
3. **Label each action** as better or worse than expected, using the value function's estimate of advantage.
4. **Retrain the policy** on all the data with that label as an extra input, then ask it at run time for the "better" behaviour.
5. Repeat.

This is offline RL, iterated: Week 9's methods, with new data between rounds. It is forgiving, since a bad hyperparameter means retraining on stored data, not re-running robots.

<SmallBesideLarge />

Learning online, step by step on the robot, is more data-efficient in principle and harder in practice: the model is huge, it has no critic, and its diffusion head and action chunks do not fit standard algorithms. Training a small policy beside the frozen large one avoids all three problems, and an off-policy actor-critic from Week 7 can then learn in tens of episodes.

## The course on one page

Every method in these sixteen weeks answers four questions about a problem: is there a model, are there demonstrations, can the agent gather its own data, and where does the reward come from.

<WhichTool />

| Family | Learns | Data it can use | Weeks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Dynamic programming, tree search | Values, by looking ahead | A model | 1, 12 |
| Q-learning, DQN | $Q^*$ | Any experience, replayed | 3, 4 |
| Policy gradient, PPO | The policy directly | Fresh experience from the current policy | 5, 6 |
| Actor-critic (SAC and relatives) | Policy and $Q^\pi$ | Any experience, replayed | 7 |
| Behaviour cloning, DAgger | The policy, from an expert | Demonstrations | 8 |
| Offline RL | Policy and values, cautiously | A fixed dataset | 9 |
| Model-based RL | A model, then any of the above | Real experience, multiplied | 12 |

And a short list of ideas that kept coming back under different names:

- **Bootstrapping**: learn a guess from a guess (TD, Q-learning, critics).
- **Stay close**: to the last policy (TRPO, PPO), to the data (offline RL), to the reference model (RLHF), to real states (model rollouts).
- **Optimism where you are ignorant, pessimism where you cannot check**: exploration bonuses when you can try things, conservative values when you cannot.
- **Relabel and reuse**: replay buffers, importance weights, hindsight goals.
- **Search and learning**: spend computation both before acting and while acting (AlphaZero, MPC, reasoning models).

## What is still open

- **Rewards that cannot be checked.** Most things worth doing have no verifier. Learned rewards are exploitable, and we lack a general remedy.
- **Using prior knowledge.** How best to combine web-scale pretraining with an agent's own experience is unsettled, for language models and robots alike.
- **World models.** Learned simulators good enough to train in would change the economics of robot learning. They are improving quickly and are not there yet.
- **Scale.** Value-based and off-policy methods are the most data-efficient and the least stable at large scale.
- **Evaluation.** A policy that works 95% of the time and one that works 99% look the same until you run hundreds of real trials.

<Recap>

- Robots make data expensive and reliability essential. Cheap experience comes from **simulation** and from **pretrained policies**; RL closes the gap each leaves.
- The **reality gap** has parametric parts (wrong numbers) and non-parametric parts (missing physics).
- **Domain randomisation** trains one robust policy over many simulators; too wide a range makes it cautious. **Learning to adapt** infers the hidden physics from recent history, usually by a privileged **teacher** and an imitating **student**. **Real-to-sim** fixes the simulator from real data.
- Imitation-trained robot policies level off around 80%. A task of five 80% stages succeeds a third of the time; five 99% stages, 95%.
- **Imitating your own successes** always improves the policy, by $\operatorname{Var}(q)/J$, which is slow near the top. **Advantage-conditioned** retraining with a fitted value function is sharper and remains supervised learning: iterated offline RL.
- For online improvement, train a **small policy beside the frozen large one**.
- The whole course is four questions: model, demonstrations, interaction, reward.

</Recap>
