## Three things worth estimating

- $V^\pi(s)$: expected future reward from $s$, following $\pi$.
- $Q^\pi(s, a)$: the same, but taking action $a$ first.
- $A^\pi(s, a) = Q^\pi(s, a) - V^\pi(s)$, the **advantage**: how much better $a$ is than what the policy usually does there.

They are tied together by $V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi}[Q^\pi(s, a)]$. The policy gradient wants the advantage:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i} \sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_{i,t} \mid s_{i,t})\; \hat A^{\pi}(s_{i,t}, a_{i,t})
$$

Fitting only $V$ is enough, because one real step turns it into an advantage: $\hat A(s_t, a_t) = r_t + \gamma \hat V(s_{t+1}) - \hat V(s_t)$. That is the TD error from Week 2, now doing a new job.

## Fitting the critic

Training the critic is supervised learning: inputs are states, labels are some estimate of the return. The choice of label is the one from Weeks 2 and 6.

| Label for $\hat V(s_t)$ | Name | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| $\sum_{t' \ge t} \gamma^{t'-t} r_{t'}$ | Monte Carlo | unbiased, noisy |
| $r_t + \gamma \hat V(s_{t+1})$ | bootstrapped (TD) | steady, biased by the critic's own errors |
| $\sum_{t'=t}^{t+n-1} \gamma^{t'-t} r_{t'} + \gamma^n \hat V(s_{t+n})$ | $n$-step | in between; the lecture notes this often works best |

The on-policy algorithm, one iteration: collect a batch with the current policy; fit $\hat V$; compute $\hat A$ for every step; take a policy gradient step with those advantages.

## How off-policy can we go?

<OffPolicySpectrum />

The first step along the spectrum was last week: reuse one batch for several gradient steps, with importance weights and a clip to keep the policy close. That is PPO. The lecture calls it "starting to be off-policy".

The next step is much bigger: keep **every** transition ever collected in a replay buffer, and train on random minibatches from it.

## Why the critic has to change

Swap a replay buffer into the algorithm above and it quietly breaks. The lecture asks the right question: when we fit $\hat V$ on all the data in the buffer, *which policy* is it the value function of?

2. Read the third card: 0.89. The buffer contains enough of both actions to learn Q for each, and the current policy's preferences do the rest.

</TryThis>

<PlainWords>

A reward in the buffer is tied to the action an old policy chose. $V(s)$ has no way to say "but I would have chosen differently". $Q(s, a)$ takes the action as an input, so each stored transition is a valid example for the action that was actually taken, no matter who took it.

</PlainWords>

## The off-policy critic and actor

<QTarget />

The critic is trained on $Q^{\pi_\theta}(s, a) \approx r + \gamma\, Q^{\pi_\theta}(s', \bar a')$ with $\bar a' \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s')$. Compare with Q-learning: the same target, with the policy's own action in place of the max.

The actor changes too. The stored actions came from old policies, so do not ask how good *they* were. Ask the critic about actions the *current* policy would take in the stored states:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_i \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(\bar a_i \mid s_i)\; \hat Q(s_i, \bar a_i), \qquad s_i \text{ from the buffer},\ \bar a_i \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_i)
$$

Using $\hat Q$ directly, with no baseline subtracted, is noisier per sample. That is acceptable because actions from the current policy cost nothing to sample, and the replay buffer supplies far more states to average over.

## Soft actor-critic

**SAC** is the practical algorithm built this way, and the one the lecture points to. Three additions beyond the above are worth knowing:

- An **entropy bonus**: the policy is rewarded for staying random, weighted by a temperature $\alpha$. This keeps it exploring without an ε.
- The **reparameterisation trick**: for a Gaussian policy, write the action as $a = \mu_\theta(s) + \sigma_\theta(s)\,\epsilon$ and differentiate $\hat Q$ straight through it, a lower-variance gradient than the score function.
- Stabilisers in the spirit of Week 4: a target network for the critic, and usually two critics with the smaller one used in the target, a cousin of Double DQN's remedy for overestimation.

## Choosing between them

The lecture's practical guidance, reworded:

| | PPO | SAC | DQN |
|---|---|---|---|
| Data | each batch a few times | replay buffer | replay buffer |
| Critic | $V^\pi$ | $Q^\pi$ | $Q^*$ |
| Main strength | stable, works out of the box | data-efficient | simple for discrete actions |
| Main weakness | needs a lot of experience | more tuning, less stable | awkward with continuous actions |
| Reach for it when | simulation is cheap | real-world data is costly | actions are few and discrete |

Two further points from the lecture. PPO with a Monte Carlo critic does not lean on the Markov property; replay-buffer methods lean on it heavily. And both can be given a head start with demonstrations: to initialise the policy for PPO, or to pre-fill the buffer for SAC. Using demonstrations is next week's subject.

<Recap>

- **Actor-critic**: the actor is the policy; the critic estimates value so that each action can be judged by an **advantage** instead of a single noisy return.
- With $\hat V$, the one-step advantage is $r + \gamma \hat V(s') - \hat V(s)$. The critic's labels can be Monte Carlo, bootstrapped or $n$-step.
- The critic matters most when returns are noisy: it averages over outcomes that a sampled return takes at face value.
- Methods form a spectrum: policy gradient (one step per batch), PPO (several), off-policy actor-critic and Q-learning (replay buffer).
- With a replay buffer, $\hat V$ would describe the old policies that filled it. Fit **$Q$** instead, with the next action drawn from the **current** policy, and train the actor on the current policy's actions too.
- Off-policy learning needs **coverage** of the actions the current policy takes.
- **SAC** adds an entropy bonus, the reparameterisation trick and stabilised critics. PPO trades data efficiency for stability; SAC the reverse.

</Recap>
