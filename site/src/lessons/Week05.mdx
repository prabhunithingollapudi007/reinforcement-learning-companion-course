So far the policy was a by-product: learn values, then act greedily on them. This week turns that around. Give the policy its own parameters $\theta$, and adjust them directly to make expected return larger. The surprise is that this can be done with nothing but sampled episodes: no model, no value function, not even a differentiable reward.

<MethodFamilies />

<Refresher title="Gradient ascent and the log-derivative identity">

To maximize $J(\theta)$, repeatedly step in the direction of its gradient: $\theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha \nabla_\theta J(\theta)$. One identity does most of the work this week: for any positive function, $\nabla_\theta p(\theta) = p(\theta)\, \nabla_\theta \log p(\theta)$, which is just the chain rule for $\log$ rearranged.

</Refresher>

## Why learn the policy directly

Three reasons, the first two practical. With continuous actions, $\arg\max_a Q(s, a)$ is itself an optimization problem, while a policy network can simply output an action. Behaviour changes smoothly with $\theta$, with none of the sudden flips a greedy policy makes when two Q-values cross (smooth is not the same as small, as Week 6 shows). And the third: sometimes the best policy is random, and a greedy policy never is.

## The objective

Write the policy as $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ and let $\tau = (s_0, a_0, r_0, \dots)$ be a whole episode with total reward $R(\tau)$. The quantity to maximize is the value of the policy:

$$
V(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta} \big[ R(\tau) \big] = \sum_\tau P(\tau; \theta)\, R(\tau)
$$

$\theta$ appears only inside $P(\tau; \theta)$: changing the policy changes which episodes are likely, not what any episode is worth.

## The gradient, from samples

Differentiate, then apply the log-derivative identity to turn the result back into an expectation:

$$
\nabla_\theta V(\theta) = \sum_\tau \nabla_\theta P(\tau; \theta)\, R(\tau) = \sum_\tau P(\tau; \theta)\, R(\tau)\, \nabla_\theta \log P(\tau; \theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau} \big[ R(\tau)\, \nabla_\theta \log P(\tau; \theta) \big]
$$

An expectation can be estimated by sampling. What is left is $\nabla_\theta \log P(\tau; \theta)$, which looks as if it needs the dynamics.

<TrajectoryFactors />

Putting it together, with $m$ sampled episodes:

$$
\nabla_\theta V(\theta) \approx \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} R(\tau^{(i)}) \sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta\big(a_t^{(i)} \mid s_t^{(i)}\big)
$$

This is the **likelihood-ratio** or **score-function** policy gradient, and the algorithm that uses it is **REINFORCE**.

<TryScoreShift />

<PlainWords>

Run the policy. For each action it took, ask "how would I change θ to make that action more likely?" Then make that change in proportion to how well the episode went. Good episodes pull the policy toward what it did; bad ones pull less, or push away.

</PlainWords>

The score $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ has a simple form for the two standard policy classes:

| Policy | Used for | Score function |
|---|---|---|
| Softmax over $\phi(s,a)^\top \theta$ | discrete actions | $\phi(s, a) - \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[\phi(s, \cdot)]$ |
| Gaussian with mean $\mu_\theta(s)$ | continuous actions | $\dfrac{(a - \mu_\theta(s))}{\sigma^2} \nabla_\theta \mu_\theta(s)$ |

For a neural-network policy, automatic differentiation computes it.

## Reducing variance

The estimator is unbiased and very noisy. Two changes cut the noise without adding bias.

**Use only future rewards.** An action cannot affect rewards that came before it.

<RewardToGo />

$$
\nabla_\theta V(\theta) \approx \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i} \sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \Big( \sum_{t' \ge t} r_{t'} \Big)
$$

**Subtract a baseline.** Replace the reward-to-go $G_t$ with $G_t - b(s_t)$, where $b$ depends on the state but not on the action. A good choice is the expected return from that state, so each action is judged against what usually happens there.

<BaselineSpread />

<TryThis>

1. At the starting settings the two rows are centred on the same place, the true gradient 0.26. The top row's spread is about 0.75; the bottom row's is 0.11.
2. Raise the constant to +5. The top row's spread grows to about 1.7 while the bottom row does not move: fifteen times less noise.
3. Set the constant to 0. The baseline still helps, 0.16 against 0.11, because rewards near their average carry little information either way.
4. Raise the batch size. Both rows tighten: more samples is the other way to cut noise, and the expensive one.

</TryThis>

<Derivation title="A baseline does not bias the gradient" why="it is two lines, and the same fact is what lets Week 7 subtract a learned value function and judge each action by its advantage.">

For any $b(s)$ that does not depend on the action,

$$
\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_\theta} \big[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, b(s) \big] = b(s) \sum_a \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s) = b(s) \sum_a \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a \mid s) = b(s)\, \nabla_\theta 1 = 0 .
$$

The probabilities sum to 1 whatever $\theta$ is, so the gradient of their sum is zero. The baseline term adds nothing to the expectation and can only change the variance.

</Derivation>

## The policy gradient theorem

The same result holds more generally, with the return replaced by the action value:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta} \big[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\; Q^{\pi_\theta}(s, a) \big]
$$

REINFORCE uses the sampled reward-to-go as an unbiased estimate of $Q^{\pi_\theta}(s, a)$. Replacing it with a learned estimate is the actor-critic idea of Week 7.

<Bridge title="how it is written in code">

CS 224R points out that nobody implements the gradient formula by hand. You build a scalar whose gradient is the estimator and let automatic differentiation do the rest:

$$
\text{loss}(\theta) = - \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i} \sum_t \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \cdot \text{weight}_t, \qquad \text{weight}_t = G_t - b(s_t) \ \text{treated as a constant.}
$$

It is the cross-entropy loss of imitation learning with each sample weighted by how well it did. The same lecture also notes the catch that drives next week: every gradient step needs **fresh** episodes from the current policy.

</Bridge>

## What breaks next

Policy gradients are simple and general, and they have two weaknesses. They are **on-policy**: data from an old policy cannot be reused as it stands, so each update costs new episodes. And the **step size** is treacherous: too small and nothing happens, too large and the policy lands somewhere bad, collects bad data, and cannot recover. Week 6 is about taking the largest step that is still safe.

<Recap>

- **Policy-based** methods parameterize the policy $\pi_\theta$ and ascend the gradient of expected return. They handle continuous actions and can learn stochastic policies.
- The **likelihood-ratio trick**: $\nabla_\theta V = \mathbb{E}[R(\tau)\, \nabla_\theta \log P(\tau; \theta)]$, and the dynamics drop out of $\nabla_\theta \log P$, leaving a sum of **score functions** $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)$.
- **REINFORCE**: sample episodes, weight each action's score by the return, step.
- In words: make actions that were followed by high return more likely.
- Variance reduction without bias: use the **reward-to-go**, and subtract a **baseline** that depends only on the state.
- The **policy gradient theorem**: $\nabla_\theta J = \mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, Q^{\pi_\theta}(s, a)]$.
- Weaknesses: on-policy, so sample-hungry, and sensitive to step size.

</Recap>
